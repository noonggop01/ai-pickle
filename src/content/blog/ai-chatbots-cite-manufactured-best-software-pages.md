You ask an AI chatbot for the "best project management software" or the "top AI writing tools," and it gives you a confident, tidy answer with sources. Feels solid, right? A recent report on manufactured recommendation sources suggests you might want to slow down before trusting that list.

![Illustration of a content assembly line churning out identical 'best software' pages with different keywords](/ai-pickle/images/blog/ai-chatbots-cite-manufactured-best-software-pages/inline-1.jpg)

The report found that three websites collectively generated 215,128 "best software" pages — not written one at a time by people who tested anything, but produced at industrial scale, templated, and optimized to rank. And Perplexity, one of the more widely used AI answer engines, was citing them as sources in its responses.

That's not a small glitch. It's a preview of how AI-generated recommendations can quietly launder low-effort content into something that looks like independent research.

## How a "Best Of" Page Farm Actually Works

The pattern behind mass-produced comparison content isn't new — it's the same playbook that flooded Google search results for years — but it matters more now because AI chatbots present their answers with an aura of authority that a normal search results page doesn't have.

Here's roughly how it works:

1. A site identifies thousands of software categories and long-tail keyword variations ("best CRM for real estate agents," "best CRM for solo consultants," "best CRM for nonprofits," and so on, multiplied endlessly).
2. Instead of hands-on testing, the site pulls product descriptions from vendor marketing pages, affiliate networks, or other existing lists.
3. A template gets filled in — headline, five to ten tools, a paragraph each, an affiliate link, maybe a comparison table — and published.
4. This gets repeated across tens of thousands of near-identical pages, tweaking only the category and keyword.

None of this requires anyone to have opened the software once. The goal isn't to inform you — it's to rank in search and, increasingly, to get scraped and cited by AI systems that summarize the web instead of sending you to it.

The scale is the tell. A legitimate review outlet might publish a few hundred deeply researched comparison pieces a year. Producing over 215,000 "best of" pages is not something a team of human reviewers does — it's an assembly line.

## Why This Matters More Now Than It Did for Google

When this kind of content farm shows up in a Google search, you still see the URL, you can eyeball the domain, and you've developed some instinct for spotting a sketchy affiliate site. AI chatbots strip most of that context away.

When Perplexity, ChatGPT with browsing, or similar tools answer "what's the best X software," they typically:

- Pull from a handful of sources without showing you how those sources were selected
- Present the answer as a synthesized, seemingly neutral conclusion
- Bury citations in small footnote-style links you may never click
- Give equal-sounding weight to a thin affiliate page and a genuinely tested review

## What This Looks Like From the Reader's Side

If you've ever asked an AI chatbot to recommend software and gotten a list that felt oddly generic — same five tools you'd expect, brief descriptions that read like marketing copy, no mention of actual downsides — there's a decent chance you were looking at a summarized version of one of these mass-produced pages.

A few signs the underlying source might be manufactured rather than tested:

| Sign | What it suggests |
|---|---|
| Every tool gets a similarly glowing, short paragraph | Template-filled content, not independent evaluation |
| No mention of pricing quirks, bugs, or limitations | Likely copied from vendor marketing, not hands-on use |
| The same five tools appear across dozens of "best X for Y" variants | Category names were swapped, but the list wasn't re-researched |
| Comparison table has suspiciously identical formatting to other "best of" lists you've seen | Same content template, different keyword |
| Affiliate links dominate, but no disclosure of testing methodology | Revenue-first, not reader-first |

![Side-by-side comparison of a generic templated software review versus a detailed hands-on review](/ai-pickle/images/blog/ai-chatbots-cite-manufactured-best-software-pages/inline-2.jpg)


None of this means every AI-cited source is fake, or that every "best of" list is worthless. But it does mean the citation itself — "according to [website]" — isn't proof of quality. It's proof that a crawler found the page and an algorithm decided it was relevant enough to summarize.

## What AI Companies Say They're Doing About It

Perplexity and similar companies have talked publicly about wanting to prioritize higher-quality, more authoritative sources, and about building in citation transparency so users can check where an answer came from [SOURCE NEEDED]. In practice, "authoritative" is hard to define at scale, and content farms have gotten good at mimicking the signals that used to indicate quality — structured data, confident tone, comparison tables, even fake-looking "methodology" sections.

The uncomfortable truth is that this is a cat-and-mouse problem, not a solved one. As long as AI answer engines rely heavily on crawling and summarizing existing web content rather than doing original evaluation, they inherit the web's existing spam problem — just with a more trustworthy-looking wrapper on top.

## How to Actually Vet a Software Recommendation

If you're choosing a tool based on an AI-generated answer, a little extra friction goes a long way:

1. **Ask the chatbot to name its sources explicitly**, not just show a footnote. Follow up with "what website is this from?"
2. **Check whether the source discloses a testing methodology.** Real reviews usually explain how something was evaluated, not just what it does.
3. **Look for specifics that only come from actual use** — a mention of a confusing settings menu, a slow export process, a support ticket that took three days. Marketing copy doesn't include friction like that.
4. **Cross-reference with a second, independent source** that isn't part of the same content network (surprisingly common — some of these "independent" sites are owned by the same parent company).
5. **Read one or two user reviews on G2, Capterra, or Reddit** for a gut check against the AI's summary.

## FAQ

**Does this mean Perplexity is unreliable?**
Not entirely — Perplexity's value is largely in speed and citation transparency compared to a plain chatbot answer with no sources at all. The issue is that citation transparency only helps if the cited sources themselves are trustworthy, and this report suggests a meaningful chunk of them aren't.

**How can I tell if a "best software" list was actually tested?**
Look for specific, sometimes unflattering details — pricing changes, a feature that didn't work as advertised, a comparison of real trade-offs rather than only benefits. Generic praise applied evenly across every product is a red flag.

**Are all AI-cited "best of" lists manufactured content?**
No. Plenty of legitimate outlets, including ones that actually run the software before writing about it, also get cited by AI tools. The problem is that AI answers currently don't reliably distinguish between the two.

**What should software vendors and reviewers do about this?**
Publishing clear methodology, dated hands-on notes, and specific pros/cons — the kind of detail a template can't fake — is currently one of the few things that reliably separates real reviews from manufactured ones, both for human readers and for the AI systems summarizing them.
